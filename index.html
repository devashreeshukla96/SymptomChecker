Healthcare strategy & operations · Product case study

Helping patients find the right level of care — not just an answer.

CareCompass is a working prototype of a care-navigation tool: a patient describes their symptoms, and the tool suggests likely conditions and the care setting that fits them — self-care, primary care, urgent care, or emergency. The interesting problem here isn't the matching algorithm; it's the operational one underneath it: how do you route people to appropriate, lower-cost, faster care without creating clinical risk?

01 The operational problem

Most patients don't have a good mental model of where to go for care. Mild symptoms end up in emergency departments; genuinely urgent symptoms get delayed by a slow primary-care scheduling process. Neither outcome is good — for the patient, or for the system absorbing the cost of a mismatch.

Industry analyses (including a widely cited 2019 UnitedHealth Group study) have estimated that a meaningful share of emergency-department visits — often cited in the range of a quarter — involve conditions that could reasonably have been treated in urgent care or a retail clinic at a fraction of the cost. Whatever the precise number in a given system, the pattern shows up in almost every health system's utilization data: acuity and setting are frequently mismatched, and it's rarely due to patients being careless — it's because the health system doesn't give them a good way to self-triage.

Why this matters for strategy & ops, not just clinical teams

  • ED overutilization drives avoidable cost and crowds capacity needed for true emergencies.
  • Under-triage (staying home with something serious) drives delayed diagnosis and worse outcomes.
  • A "digital front door" that routes well is a lever for cost, access, and satisfaction at once — which is exactly why health systems and payers keep building these tools.

02 The approach — and a deliberate simplification

This prototype intentionally does not use a black-box machine learning model to make the triage call. That's a design decision, not a limitation I ran out of time to fix.

1. Symptom matching

The demo matches free-text symptom descriptions against a library of documented symptom patterns for 66 conditions, entirely in the browser — nothing is sent to a server.

2. Care-pathway taxonomy

Every condition is pre-classified into one of four care tiers by triage logic that a clinical operations team would sign off on — not inferred by a model at run time.

3. Safety-first override

Red-flag symptoms (chest pain, stroke signs, severe bleeding) short-circuit the whole matcher and immediately recommend emergency care, regardless of what else was typed.

Why keep it this simple? Because in a real deployment, the hard part isn't building a classifier — it's building something a clinical governance committee, a legal team, and a skeptical clinician can all audit and trust. A rule-based system where every recommendation traces back to an explicit, reviewable decision is a more honest starting point for that conversation than a model that's more "accurate" on paper but can't explain itself. Explainability and auditability are themselves operational requirements, not just engineering nice-to-haves.

04 The care-pathway framework

Every condition in this prototype maps to one of four tiers. This taxonomy — not the text matching — is the actual design artifact.

Self-care

Monitor at home. Mild, self-limiting symptoms.

Target: no visit needed

e.g. common cold, mild allergies, acne

Primary care / telehealth

Non-urgent, but needs a clinician and likely labs or a treatment plan.

Target: within days

e.g. hypertension follow-up, hypothyroidism, chronic joint pain

Urgent care

Needs in-person evaluation soon, but isn't immediately life-threatening.

Target: same day

e.g. gout flare, suspected UTI complication, dehydration

Emergency

Potentially life-threatening. Call emergency services immediately.

Target: now

e.g. heart attack, stroke signs, pancreatitis

05 How you'd know this was working

If this shipped inside a real health system, a strategy/ops team wouldn't measure "accuracy" in isolation — they'd track the operational and safety outcomes that justify the investment.

ED diversion rate

% of users who would default to the ED but are appropriately routed to a lower-acuity setting instead.

Under-triage / miss rate

% of emergent presentations not flagged as emergency — the single most important safety metric, and the one you optimize hardest against, even at the cost of over-flagging.

Time-to-appropriate-care

Elapsed time from symptom onset to reaching the recommended setting, vs. a baseline without the tool.

Follow-through rate

% of users who actually book/attend the recommended setting rather than abandoning the flow.

Cost per avoided ED visit

Blended savings from successful diversions, net of the tool's operating cost and any redirected-but-should-have-gone cases.

Equity of routing

Whether recommendation quality and follow-through hold steady across language, income, and access-to-transport segments.

06 Limitations & responsible design

  • This is not a diagnostic device and has not been through any clinical validation, IRB review, or regulatory process a real product would require.
  • The condition list (66 entries) and care-tier mapping were built by hand for this prototype — they are illustrative of the approach, not a clinically vetted taxonomy.
  • Symptom matching is simple keyword overlap. It does not account for symptom severity, duration, patient history, age, or comorbidities — all of which change real triage decisions.
  • No accommodation yet for pediatric or geriatric presentations, which often differ meaningfully from the adult-symptom patterns used here.
  • A real version would need a named clinical governance owner, a defined liability and escalation path, and an explicit plan for the false-negative case — someone who needed emergency care and wasn't told so.
  • Runs entirely client-side by design: no symptom data is transmitted or stored anywhere. A production version handling real patient data would need a full privacy and security review.

07 What I'd do next with real resources

  1. Validate the taxonomy. Sit down with a clinical advisor (nurse triage lead or ED physician) and pressure-test every self-care and primary-care classification — those are the ones with the most room for error.
  2. Calibrate against real utilization data. Compare recommended pathways against actual historical visit outcomes to estimate a realistic diversion rate before ever touting one.
  3. Design for the miss case. Build an explicit "if unsure, escalate" bias into the logic, and instrument it so under-triage events are reviewable after the fact.
  4. Make it a conversation, not a form. Real triage lines ask follow-up questions (duration, severity, associated symptoms) rather than taking one input and returning a list.
  5. Integrate with the actual next step. Connect the recommended pathway to a real provider directory, telehealth booking flow, or nurse line — the recommendation is only useful if it's actionable.
  6. Test for equity, not just accuracy. Run the tool against symptom phrasing that varies by language proficiency and health literacy, and measure whether quality holds up.

08 About this project

This project started from an open-source NLP symptom-checker template (a Flask app backed by an LSTM intent classifier — you can still find that original prototype in /legacy-prototype in the repo). I redesigned it into this case study to make a different point: the hard, interesting problem in this space isn't the model, it's the care-pathway design and the operational judgment behind it — which is the lens I want to bring to healthcare strategy and operations work.

Everything on this page — the care-pathway taxonomy, the safety-override logic, the framing of the problem in operational terms — was built for this reframing. The interactive demo runs entirely as static HTML/CSS/JavaScript, hosted on GitHub Pages, with the condition data compiled from the original project's dataset.
